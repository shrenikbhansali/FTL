\begin{abstract}
Federated transfer fine-tuning (FTL) of large language models (LLMs) faces severe heterogeneity: when clients correspond to distinct tasks or domains, naive aggregation (e.g., FedAvg) can cancel transferable signal and amplify task-private interference. We introduce \textbf{SubspaceBank}, a geometry-aware federation method for LoRA-based LLM adaptation in the dataset-pure FTL regime. At each communication round, the server constructs low-rank orthonormal bases from client update matrices and decomposes each client’s update into shared (global), client-private, and residual components. SubspaceBank aggregates by reweighting these components to emphasize consensus-aligned directions while shrinking residuals. Across heterogeneous-client settings, SubspaceBank yields stronger global robustness and improved stability than FedAvg under comparable LoRA parameterization, highlighting subspace decomposition as a practical primitive for federated LLM transfer learning.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Fine-tuning Large language models (LLMs) for new tasks and domains is rising in popularity, but in many  settings the data required for adaptation cannot be pooled. Federated learning (FL) offers a natural alternative: clients keep data local and share only model updates. 

Most existing federated LLM work, however, is organized around a a single global objective or task under mild non-IIDness, where averaging remains a reasonable approximation. In this paper we study a different and practically common regime: \emph{federated transfer fine-tuning (FTL)}, where each client corresponds to a distinct dataset/task/domain, heterogeneity is intrinsic and persistent, and the goal is both a single global model that performs robustly across all constituent tasks, and clients that retain their performance on their training task. Despite the popularity of LLMs in multiple domains, \textbf{there are no prior works that directly study federated transfer learning for LLMs}. This creates a gap: we lack both methods and evaluation protocols for building one globally reliable LLM from heterogeneous, non-mixable client datasets.

The core problem in FTL is interference. Even when each client makes local progress, client updates can point in incompatible directions: a direction that improves one task can degrade others. Under strong task diversity, naïvely averaging LLM parameters can cancel transferable signal while unintentionally preserving or non-transferable components, producing unstable training and poor global robustness. We demonstrate this experimentally, FedAvg underperforms centralized multi-task training on multiple benchmarks (\Cref{sec:exp-global}), motivating our method.

We propose \textbf{SubspaceBank}, a geometry-aware federation method designed for LoRA-based FTL. SubspaceBank is built around a simple principle: heterogeneous client deltas are mixtures of \emph{shared} structure (directions likely to transfer), \emph{private} structure (task-specific directions), and \emph{residual} components (noise, mismatch, or unstable directions). 

Instead of averaging raw deltas, SubspaceBank decomposes each participating client update into these components and aggregates them with different semantics. Concretely, at each round, the server stacks client update matrices and computes a low-rank orthonormal \emph{shared} basis. For each client, it then extracts a \emph{private} basis from the residual after removing the shared span, yielding a shared/private/residual decomposition. The server forms a structured update by amplifying consensus-aligned directions and shrinking residual components, while preserving private structure orthogonal to the shared span. 

SubspaceBank addresses interference by decomposing client deltas into shared, private, and residual components and reweighting them during aggregation, aligning the federated procedure with the geometry induced by heterogeneous LoRA deltas.

Across heterogeneous-client settings, SubspaceBank yields stronger global robustness and improved stability than FedAvg under comparable LoRA parameterization. In our experiments, SubspaceBank improves global performance on all benchmarks relative to FedAvg and substantially narrows the gap to centralized training (\Cref{sec:exp-global}). Importantly, these gains do not come at the expense of client specialization: SubspaceBank also improves or matches client-local performance across all datasets in the specialization retention evaluation (\Cref{sec:exp-specialization}). We further evaluate SubspaceBank in a Multi-Client Datasets regime with partial participation and study ablations and geometry diagnostics to isolate which components matter and how shared/private/residual structure evolves during training (\Cref{sec:exp-ablations,sec:exp-geometry}).

Our work makes three main contributions: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item We formalize and study \emph{federated transfer fine-tuning for LLMs} in a dataset-pure regime where the objective is global robustness across heterogeneous tasks.
    \item We introduce \textbf{SubspaceBank}, a residual-first shared/private/residual decomposition and structured aggregation rule for LoRA deltas.
    \item We provide an evaluation protocol spanning Single-Client and Multi-Client dataset-pure regimes, including global robustness and specialization retention metrics, and accompanying diagnostics and ablations.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

\subsection{Federated Learning for LLM Fine-Tuning}
Deploying large language models (LLMs) in federated learning (FL) is shaped by two practical constraints. First, \emph{communication} is a major bottleneck: exchanging full model parameters or dense updates is costly at LLM scale, so systems often turn to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods to reduce communication cost. Second, \emph{optimization can be fragile} under non-IID client data and multi-step local training \citet{mcmahan2017communication,reddi2020adaptive}. Consequently, many systems often limit heterogeneity and/or local training budgets in practice.

\subsection{Federated Transfer Learning Under Heterogeneity}
Classical FL aggregates client updates to optimize a global objective. Under non-IID data, heterogeneity can produce \emph{client drift}: local optimization on different client objectives moves parameters in incompatible directions, so naive averaging may cancel progress, amplify variance, and yield unstable training dynamics (e.g., \citet{mcmahan2017communication,stich2018local,karimireddy2020scaffold}). These issues are often formalized through gradient dissimilarity or bounded drift assumptions and manifest as degraded global generalization.

Federated transfer learning (FTL) makes heterogeneity structural rather than incidental: each client corresponds to a distinct dataset, task, or domain, and the target is a \emph{single} model that transfers across the full client/task set. In this regime, the central failure mode is \emph{interference}. Client updates typically mix (i) transferable components that would benefit many tasks with (ii) task-private components that improve one task while harming others. Aggregating such mixed updates can suppress shared signal through cancellation while increasing the effective noise of the global step, leading to brittle across-task behavior. FTL places stronger pressure on mechanisms that preserve cross-task signal while controlling negative transfer, and remains underexplored for LLM fine-tuning.

\subsection{PEFT and LoRA}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) adapts a pretrained model by training only a small set of parameters while freezing the backbone. In FL, PEFT reduces the communication footprint of updates and the amount of optimizer state maintained on clients.

LoRA~\citet{hu2022lora} is a widely used PEFT mechanism. For a linear transformation with weight matrix $W\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$, LoRA introduces a low-rank update parameterized as
\[
\Delta W \;=\; B A,\qquad
A\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times d_{\mathrm{in}}},\;\;
B\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times r},
\]
and uses the effective weight $W+\Delta W$ during forward passes while keeping the pretrained $W$ frozen. The trainable parameters are the adapter matrices $(A,B)$ (and optional scaling), where the rank $r\ll \min(d_{\mathrm{in}},d_{\mathrm{out}})$ controls the trainable capacity.

In a federated setting, clients perform local optimization over LoRA parameters and communicate updates back to the server. It is therefore useful to view the communicated information as \emph{matrix-shaped deltas} associated with identifiable parameter tensors (e.g., the LoRA $A$ or $B$ matrices attached to a particular module). We will refer to an individual trainable 2D tensor by a \emph{key}---an exact parameter name in the model state---and we will write client updates for such a key as matrix deltas.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Our goal is to learn a single global LLM that is robust across heterogeneous client tasks in a federated transfer fine-tuning (FTL) regime. At each round, for each banked key, the server computes a shared right-subspace from stacked client deltas, a private right-subspace per client from shared-removed residuals, forms structured deltas, and aggregates.

\subsection{Notation and Preliminaries}
\label{sec:method-protocol}

We consider $N$ clients indexed by $i\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ and training rounds $t=0,\dots,T-1$. Each client $i$ has local data distribution $\mathcal{D}_i$ and corresponding objective $f_i(W)$ (e.g., supervised fine-tuning loss). In FTL, the goal is to obtain a single model that is robust across the set of client tasks/benchmarks, rather than optimizing for a single shared distribution.

At round $t$, the server samples a participating set $S_t$ ($|S_t|\leq N$) and broadcasts the current global parameters $W^t$ to clients in $S_t$. Each participating client performs $E$ local optimizer steps of LoRA fine-tuning, starting from $W^t$, producing updated client parameters $W_i^t$. The server aggregates these updates to obtain $W^{t+1}$.

We describe aggregation at the level of individual parameter \emph{keys}. Let $k$ denote an exact state-dict parameter name corresponding to a 2D tensor eligible for SubspaceBank. For each banked key $k$ and participating client $i\in S_t$, we define the (matrix-valued) client delta
\begin{equation}
  D_{k,i}^t \triangleq W_{k,i}^t - W_k^t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}.
  \label{eq:delta_def}
\end{equation}
We use weights $w_i$ for aggregation (uniform or proportional to client sample counts). Unless stated otherwise, $D_{k,i}^t$ refers to the raw parameter delta. For non-banked parameters, we apply standard weighted averaging; the SubspaceBank mechanism applies only to a selected subset of matrix-shaped parameters.

\subsection{Banked Keys (LoRA adapters)}
\label{sec:banked-keys-method}

SubspaceBank operates on \textbf{raw parameter deltas} (\Cref{eq:delta_def}) for selected 2D tensors. We bank LoRA adapter tensors (the $A$ and $B$ matrices defined in \Cref{sec:background}) as separate keys. Importantly, we do \emph{not} reconstruct a combined adapter update (e.g., $\Delta W = BA$), nor do we concatenate LoRA components across modules: SubspaceBank decomposes and aggregates each banked matrix key independently. We bank LoRA-only; non-banked parameters use standard weighted averaging as described in \Cref{sec:method-protocol}.

For each banked matrix of shape $(d_{\mathrm{out}}, d_{\mathrm{in}})$, we represent update geometry using \textbf{right singular vectors}, i.e., we construct subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ that capture dominant directions in the \emph{right} space. This choice yields a consistent interface for all banked matrices and aligns naturally with low-rank structure: even when $d_{\mathrm{out}}$ is large, the update often varies in a low-dimensional subset of the input-side directions. 

\subsection{SubspaceBank Overview and Design Goals}
\label{sec:method-overview}

Each round, the server receives deltas $\{D_{k,i}^t\}_{i\in S_t}$ for all banked keys $k$. For each banked key, we decompose each client delta into a shared right-subspace component, a client-private component within the shared-removed residual, and a remaining residual; we then reweight these components during aggregation. We now formalize each component.

\subsection{SubspaceBank: Shared Basis Construction}
\label{sec:shared-basis}

Fix a banked key $k$ and a round $t$ with participating set $S_t$. Each participating client provides a delta matrix $D_{k,i}^t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$. To extract consensus geometry across clients, we stack these deltas along rows:
\begin{equation}
  M_k^t \triangleq \operatorname{concat}_{i\in S_t} D_{k,i}^t
  \in\mathbb{R}^{(|S_t|d_{\mathrm{out}})\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}.
  \label{eq:stack_def}
\end{equation}
We compute a truncated singular value decomposition (SVD),
\[
  M_k^t = U_k^t \Sigma_k^t (V_k^t)^\top,
\]
and define the \emph{shared} basis $S_k^t$ using the top $r_g$ right singular vectors:
\begin{equation}
  S_k^t \triangleq V_k^t[:,1{:}r_g]\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_g},
  \qquad
  \Pi_{S_k^t}\triangleq S_k^t (S_k^t)^\top.
  \label{eq:shared_basis}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, $S_k^t$ is the solution to the orthonormal subspace approximation problem
\begin{equation}
  S_k^t \in \arg\min_{\substack{S\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_g}\\ S^\top S = I}}
  \big\|M_k^t(I-SS^\top)\big\|_F^2
  \;=\;
  \arg\max_{\substack{S\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_g}\\ S^\top S = I}}
  \big\|M_k^t S\big\|_F^2,
  \label{eq:shared_opt}
\end{equation}
so $\Pi_{S_k^t}$ captures the right-space directions that best explain the stacked updates in least-squares sense, and thus functions as a data-dependent estimate of consensus-aligned update geometry.

\subsection{SubspaceBank: Residual-First Private Basis and Delta Decomposition}
\label{sec:private-basis}

The shared basis alone is not sufficient in FTL: forcing all updates into a single shared span can distort or discard task-specific structure. SubspaceBank therefore extracts a private basis \emph{from the shared-removed residual} for each client, ensuring that private directions are (by construction) orthogonal to the shared span.

For each participating client $i\in S_t$, we define the shared component as the right-space component in $\mathrm{span}(S_k^t)$:
\begin{equation}
  D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}} \triangleq D_{k,i}^t \Pi_{S_k^t}.
  \label{eq:glob_comp}
\end{equation}
We then form the shared-removed residual
\begin{equation}
  R_{k,i}^{t,(0)} \triangleq D_{k,i}^t (I-\Pi_{S_k^t}).
  \label{eq:resid0}
\end{equation}
Next, we compute a candidate private basis from the top $r_c$ right singular vectors of $R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}$:
\[
  R_{k,i}^{t,(0)} = \tilde U_{k,i}^t \tilde \Sigma_{k,i}^t (\tilde V_{k,i}^t)^\top,
  \qquad
  \hat P_{k,i}^t \triangleq \tilde V_{k,i}^t[:,1{:}r_c].
\]
Equivalently, $\hat P_{k,i}^t$ solves the residual-subspace approximation problem
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \hat P_{k,i}^t \in\;&
  \arg\min_{\substack{P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_c}\\ P^\top P = I}}
  \big\|R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}(I-PP^\top)\big\|_F^2 \\
  =\;&
  \arg\max_{\substack{P\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times r_c}\\ P^\top P = I}}
  \big\|R_{k,i}^{t,(0)} P\big\|_F^2.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:private_opt}
\end{equation}

Since $R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}$ is already orthogonal to $S_k^t$ in the right space, $\hat P_{k,i}^t$ typically lies largely outside the shared span; we nevertheless explicitly orthogonalize against $S_k^t$ and re-orthonormalize:
\begin{equation}
  P_{k,i}^t \triangleq \operatorname{orth}\!\big((I-\Pi_{S_k^t})\hat P_{k,i}^t\big),
  \qquad
  \Pi_{P_{k,i}^t}\triangleq P_{k,i}^t (P_{k,i}^t)^\top.
  \label{eq:priv_basis}
\end{equation}
Here $\operatorname{orth}(\cdot)$ returns an orthonormal basis spanning its argument’s column space. We then define the private component \emph{within the residual}:
\begin{equation}
  D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}} \triangleq R_{k,i}^{t,(0)} \Pi_{P_{k,i}^t}.
  \label{eq:priv_comp}
\end{equation}
Finally, we define the remaining residual component
\begin{equation}
  R_{k,i}^t \triangleq D_{k,i}^t - D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}} - D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}}.
  \label{eq:final_resid}
\end{equation}

This residual-first construction is central: it prevents private structure from being ``explained away'' by the shared span, and it yields a three-way decomposition in which the shared and private components capture interpretable update geometry, while $R_{k,i}^t$ collects unmodeled directions that we explicitly shrink during aggregation.


\subsection{SubspaceBank: Structured Aggregation}
\label{sec:structured-agg}

Given the decomposition above, SubspaceBank aggregates using a structured recombination that treats shared, private, and residual components differently. For each key $k$ and participating client $i$, we define a \emph{structured delta}
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D_{k,i}^t
  \triangleq
  D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}}
  + \beta_g\, D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}}
  + \beta_r\, R_{k,i}^t,
  \label{eq:structured_delta}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_g\ge 0$ controls amplification of consensus-aligned directions and $\beta_r\in[0,1]$ controls shrinkage of residual components. The aggregated update for key $k$ is then
\begin{equation}
  \Delta_k^t \triangleq \sum_{i\in S_t} w_i\, \tilde D_{k,i}^t,
  \qquad
  W_k^{t+1} \leftarrow W_k^t + \alpha\, \Delta_k^t,
  \label{eq:agg_update}
\end{equation}
with global step size $\alpha>0$. Intuitively, $\beta_g$ increases the contribution of update directions that many clients agree on (transfer), $D^{\mathrm{priv}}$ preserves client-specific structure that is orthogonal to the shared span (avoiding a ``one-span-fits-all'' collapse), and $\beta_r$ suppresses leftover components that tend to be unstable under averaging.

\paragraph{FedAvg as a special case.}
SubspaceBank reduces to standard weighted averaging on banked keys when $\beta_g=\beta_r=1$. In this case,
\[
\tilde D_{k,i}^t = D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}} + D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}} + R_{k,i}^t = D_{k,i}^t,
\]
so the aggregated update $\Delta_k^t=\sum_{i\in S_t} w_i D_{k,i}^t$ matches FedAvg for banked parameters.


The structured update admits a simple operator interpretation that makes explicit what is being amplified, preserved, and shrunk. Suppressing indices for readability and writing $\Pi_S$ and $\Pi_P$ for the shared and private projectors, the construction implies
\begin{equation}
  \tilde D
  = \beta_r D
  + (\beta_g-\beta_r)\,D\Pi_S
  + (1-\beta_r)\,(D(I-\Pi_S))\Pi_P.
  \label{eq:operator_view}
\end{equation}
The first term scales the entire update, the second term selectively boosts the shared (consensus) component, and the third term preserves structure within the shared-removed residual that lies in the private subspace. This view clarifies that SubspaceBank is not assuming private subspaces are mutually orthogonal across clients; rather, it explicitly separates consensus-aligned and residual directions at aggregation time.

We analyze the decomposition using energy fractions of the shared/private/residual components; formal definitions appear where first used in \Cref{sec:exp-geometry}.

\subsection{Experimental Setting}
\label{sec:exp-regimes}

We consider two FTL regimes, both of which treat clients as \emph{dataset-pure}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Single-Client Datasets: There is one client per dataset/task.
    \item Multi-Client Datasets: Each dataset is distributed across multiple clients, but each client only corresponds to one dataset (no multi-task client data). 
\end{itemize}

For training data and benchmarking LLMs in a transfer learning setting, there is no standard, widely adopted benchmark suite for federated transfer fine-tuning of LLMs. We therefore propose the use of five datasets with heterogeneous tasks for training, and their respective test sets for evaluation and benchmarking:
\begin{itemize}
    \item GSM8k - We use GSM8k to represent the math task. We use accuracy on its test set as our evaluation metric. 
    \item HellaSwag - HellaSwag represents a commonsense reasoning task. We use accuracy on its test set as our evaluation metric. 
    \item XSum - XSum is an extreme summarization dataset. We use ROUGE-L F1 on its test set as our evaluation metric. 
    \item HotPotQA - HotPotQA is a multi-hop QA dataset. We use F1 on its test set as our evaluation metric. 
    \item MBPP - MBPP is a python coding dataset. We use pass@5 as our evaluation metric.
\end{itemize}

In the Single-Client Datasets regime, we evaluate the global model on all task benchmarks, and evaluate specialization retention of the constituent client models on their respective benchmarks. In the Multi-Client Datasets regime, we simply benchmark the global model due to the added system complexity and compute demand. 

We believe this is a diverse range of tasks that will clearly demonstrate any successes or failure in transfer learning. The tasks require different skills and knowledge of the LLM, and often use differing system instructions, formatting, or prompting strategies. We do \textbf{no standardization} between the datasets, maintaining any and all heterogeneity between the datasets. 

Unless otherwise stated, we fine-tune a LLaMA2-7B model with LoRA on attention q/k/v/o modules. We run for 60 federated rounds with full participation in the Single-Client regime. We perform 60 rounds with partial client participation in the Multi-Client regime. Detailed hyperparameters, hardware, and preprocessing are located in the Appendix.

\subsection{Baselines and Training Regimes}
\label{sec:exp-methods}
We principally look at four training regimes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Centralized multi-task training:} Train one LoRA model on the union of all the datasets.
  \item \textbf{Centralized single-task training:} Train one LoRA model on one of the datasets. We provide the single-task trained model for each dataset, acting as a representative ceiling for that task's performance. 
  \item \textbf{FedAvg:} standard federated averaging on LoRA parameters (and standard FedAvg on other parameters).
  \item \textbf{SubspaceBank:} SubspaceBank structured aggregation with shared/private/residual decomposition.
\end{itemize}

Many federated optimization methods are designed around a single-distribution FL objective (often under mild heterogeneity) or personalization assumptions. Adapting such methods to the dataset-pure FTL protocol and evaluating them fairly at LLM scale is nontrivial. We therefore focus comparisons on FedAvg and centralized training as primary reference points, and include extensive internal ablations to isolate the contributions of SubspaceBank’s components.

\subsection{Main Results: Global Robustness Across Benchmarks}
\label{sec:exp-global}

\paragraph{Single-Client Datasets regime (global model).}


Table~\ref{tab:global_nonsharded} reports global-model robustness after 60 rounds in the Single-Client Datasets regime. The results demonstrate the interference failure mode emphasized in \Cref{sec:intro}: FedAvg falls well below centralized multi-task training on multiple tasks, indicating that naive averaging suppresses transferable signals. In contrast, SubspaceBank recovers a large fraction of this gap and improves performance on \emph{four of five} benchmarks relative to FedAvg: GSM8K increases from 0.071 to 0.125 (+0.054), HellaSwag from 0.150 to 0.360 (+0.210), XSum from 0.164 to 0.183 (+0.019), and HotPotQA F1 from 0.124 to 0.130 (+0.006). 


A closer look suggests that SubspaceBank’s gains are broad rather than concentrated in a single benchmark: it nearly matches centralized multi-task on GSM8K (0.125 vs.\ 0.136) and substantially narrows the gap on HellaSwag (0.360 vs.\ 0.460), while slightly exceeding centralized multi-task on XSum (0.183 vs.\ 0.179). Notably, MBPP improves (0.060 $\rightarrow$ 0.110), highlighting an important limitation: some tasks may contribute predominantly task-private directions that do not transfer under a single global model, and the combination of low pass@5 rates and limited evaluation samples can make small differences appear discontinuous. 

\paragraph{Multi-Client Datasets.}
Table~\ref{tab:global_sharded_shell} reports global-model robustness in the Multi-Client Datasets regime, where each dataset is split across multiple clients and only a subset of clients participate each round. This setting compounds the core FTL difficulty: in addition to cross-task heterogeneity, the server observes a \emph{partial} and \emph{varying} view of each task’s update distribution from round to round. As a result, naive averaging is especially brittle: FedAvg collapses sharply relative to centralized multi-task training.

SubspaceBank remains effective under this more realistic systems regime. It improves over FedAvg on all five benchmarks, indicating that geometry-aware aggregation continues to recover transferable signal even when each round’s participant set provides an incomplete snapshot of the heterogeneous client population.

These across-the-board gains suggest that suppressing unstable residual components and preserving structured (shared and private) directions helps stabilize the global step under partial participation. While SubspaceBank still trails centralized multi-task training in this regime, the remaining gap is expected given that the federated setting combines strict data locality with additional stochasticity from client sampling; nevertheless, SubspaceBank substantially narrows the gap relative to FedAvg and demonstrates robustness to sharding and partial participation in dataset-pure FTL.

\subsection{Geometry Diagnostics}
\label{sec:exp-geometry}

\begin{figure}[h]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{geometry_fractions_baseline.pdf}
  \caption{Round-wise mean energy fractions for SubspaceBank in the Single-Client regime. Values are averaged over all banked LoRA keys. Private energy dominates and the shared component remains nontrivial across rounds.}
  \label{fig:geometry-fractions}
\end{figure}

We analyze the decomposition using energy fractions of the shared and private components. For each delta $D$ (suppressing indices), we compute energy fractions
\begin{align}
  \texttt{glob\_frac} &= \frac{\|D^{\mathrm{glob}}\|_F^2}{\|D\|_F^2} \\
  \texttt{priv\_frac} &= \frac{\|D^{\mathrm{priv}}\|_F^2}{\|D\|_F^2} \\
  \texttt{resid\_frac} &= \frac{\|R\|_F^2}{\|D\|_F^2} 
  \label{eq:fractions}
\end{align}

In the single-client regime, the SubspaceBank decomposition is dominated by client-private energy. Averaged across all banked LoRA keys, the round-wise global fraction is 0.228 on average (min 0.188, max 0.281), while the private fraction is 0.572 on average (min 0.519, max 0.612). \Cref{fig:geometry-fractions} plots the per-round averages, showing a modest early decline in shared energy with a corresponding rise in private energy, while a nontrivial shared component persists.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

Our initial baseline results support the hypothesis that FTL with heterogeneous dataset-clients induces destructive interference for naïve averaging: FedAvg underperforms centralized training across most benchmarks (\Cref{sec:exp-global}). SubspaceBank partially mitigates this, improving global robustness on several tasks, consistent with the idea that separating shared vs. private directions and constraining cross-private leakage can stabilize training.

We expect SubspaceBank to help most when heterogeneity is high but there exists meaningful shared structure: in such cases, \texttt{glob\_frac} should be nontrivial and amplifying $D^{\mathrm{glob}}$ should improve robustness, while suppressing residuals should reduce instability. 

Conversely, the method may help less under low heterogeneity (where FedAvg already works), in regimes with extremely small right dimension for key parameters (notably LoRA $B$), or when deltas are noisy and private structure cannot be reliably estimated from limited $S_t$. Partial participation is a key limitation as $S_k$ and $P_{k,i}$ are computed only from $S_t$ each round (no cache/EMA/TTL), basis estimates can vary across rounds as participation changes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We position SubspaceBank relative to (i) federated optimization under heterogeneity, (ii) federated adaptation of foundation models and LLMs with parameter-efficient tuning, and (iii) subspace and constraint methods for interference control in transfer and multi-task learning.

\paragraph{Federated learning under heterogeneity}
A large body of work studies how non-IID client data and multi-step local training induce client drift and unstable global updates, and proposes algorithmic stabilizers such as proximal regularization, control variates, normalization, and server-side adaptive optimization
\citet{karimireddy2020scaffold,wang2020fednova,reddi2021adaptive,acar2021feddyn}.
These methods primarily target a \emph{single} global objective and aim to make aggregation behave well despite distribution shift. A complementary line targets \emph{personalization} or \emph{multi-task} structure, e.g., by learning shared representations with task-specific heads, meta-learning personalized models, or clustering clients into latent groups
\citet{smith2017mocha,dinh2020pfedme,li2021ditto,collins2021fedrep,ghosh2020ifca,sattler2020clustered}.
While these frameworks address heterogeneity, their objectives differ from ours: in federated transfer fine-tuning (FTL), each client corresponds to a distinct dataset/task/domain and we seek a \emph{single} global model that is robust across all tasks (and client models that retain specialization). SubspaceBank is designed specifically around this transfer objective and the resulting \emph{interference} between task-private and transferable directions.

\paragraph{FL for LLMs with PEFT.}
In the LLM setting, recent systems and benchmarks typically emphasize (i) federated instruction tuning or federated alignment with broadly similar supervision across clients, (ii) user- or silo-level personalization, or (iii) systems scaling and communication efficiency for federating large models
\citet{ye2024openfedllm,liu2024ferret,zhao2022fedprompt,guo2023promptfl,hu2022lora}.
These efforts establish that federated LLM adaptation is feasible and that PEFT can be an effective communication primitive, but they generally do not isolate the dataset-pure \emph{transfer} regime we formalize—where clients are different tasks by construction and the central question is how to build one globally reliable model without cross-task negative transfer. 

SubspaceBank complements this line by targeting interference explicitly: we decompose LoRA update geometry into shared/private/residual components and incorporate per-client constraints to reduce cross-client leakage during local training.

\paragraph{Subspace and constraint methods for interference control.}
In centralized multi-task learning, gradient conflict has motivated “gradient surgery” and related conflict-averse updates that modify per-task gradients to reduce destructive interference
\citet{yu2020pcgrad,liu2021cagrad}.
In continual learning, constraint-based methods preserve prior-task performance by restricting updates to avoid previously learned directions, often via gradient constraints or orthogonality penalties
\citet{lopezpaz2017gem,chaudhry2018agem,farajtabar2020ogd}.

SubspaceBank is inspired by these geometric perspectives but differs in a key way: our shared/private subspaces are \emph{constructed from federated client deltas} each round and used to define a structured aggregation rule that amplifies consensus while shrinking residuals.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We studied federated transfer fine-tuning (FTL) for LLMs, where clients correspond to distinct datasets/tasks and the goal is one global model robust across all benchmarks. We introduced SubspaceBank, which decomposes participating client LoRA deltas into shared/private/residual components via a residual-first nested decomposition procedure and aggregates them using a structured recombination rule that amplifies consensus while suppressing residual noise. Our baseline results show that FedAvg underperforms centralized training under heterogeneous dataset-clients, while SubspaceBank improves over FedAvg on several benchmarks and narrows the gap to centralized. Ablations indicate that both decomposition and reweighting are required and A+B banking is necessary to recover full performance. A moderate weighting choice ($\beta_g,\beta_r \approx 0.5$) performs best in the current sweep. Reproducibility details, budget accounting, and extended diagnostics are provided in the Appendix.

\section{Proofs and Extended Theory Details}
\label{sec:appendix-theory}

This appendix collects the minimal linear-algebraic facts underlying SubspaceBank: (i) why the shared/private bases in \Cref{eq:shared_opt,eq:private_opt} are given by top right singular vectors, (ii) the orthogonality and energy decompositions implied by the residual-first construction, and (iii) the operator form in \Cref{eq:operator_view}.

\subsection{SVD optimality for the shared and private bases}
\label{sec:appendix-svd-opt}

We restate the shared basis optimization (\Cref{eq:shared_opt}) in a form that makes the SVD solution immediate. Fix any matrix $M\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d}$ (in our method, $M=M_k^t$) and any orthonormal $S\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}$ with $S^\top S=I$.

\paragraph{Lemma A.1 (Shared-basis optimality).}
Let $\Pi_S \triangleq SS^\top$. Then
\begin{equation}
\|M(I-\Pi_S)\|_F^2 \;=\; \|M\|_F^2 - \|MS\|_F^2,
\label{eq:appendix-pythag-ms}
\end{equation}
so minimizing $\|M(I-\Pi_S)\|_F^2$ over orthonormal $S$ is equivalent to maximizing $\|MS\|_F^2$. An optimizer is given by choosing $S$ to be the top-$r$ right singular vectors of $M$.

\paragraph{Proof.}
Using $\|A\|_F^2=\mathrm{tr}(A^\top A)$ and $\Pi_S^2=\Pi_S$, $\Pi_S^\top=\Pi_S$,
\[
\|M(I-\Pi_S)\|_F^2
= \mathrm{tr}\!\big((I-\Pi_S)M^\top M(I-\Pi_S)\big)
= \mathrm{tr}(M^\top M) - \mathrm{tr}(\Pi_S M^\top M).
\]
Since $\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_S M^\top M)=\mathrm{tr}(S^\top M^\top M S)=\|MS\|_F^2$, we obtain \Cref{eq:appendix-pythag-ms}. The maximizer of $\mathrm{tr}(S^\top M^\top M S)$ over orthonormal $S$ is the top-$r$ eigenspace of $M^\top M$, equivalently the top-$r$ right singular vectors of $M$. \hfill$\square$

Applying Lemma A.1 to $M_k^t$ yields \Cref{eq:shared_opt} and justifies $S_k^t=V_k^t[:,1{:}r_g]$ in \Cref{eq:shared_basis}. The same argument applies to the private-basis objective \Cref{eq:private_opt} with $M=R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}$, giving $\hat P_{k,i}^t$ as the top-$r_c$ right singular vectors.

\subsection{Orthogonality and energy decompositions}
\label{sec:appendix-orthogonality}

The decomposition in \Cref{eq:glob_comp,eq:priv_comp,eq:final_resid} is built by right-multiplying by orthogonal projectors. This implies exact orthogonality identities in the Frobenius inner product, which in turn justify the energy-fraction diagnostics in \Cref{sec:exp-geometry}.

\paragraph{Lemma A.2 (Right-projector Pythagoras).}
Let $\Pi$ be any orthogonal projector in $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{in}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$ (i.e., $\Pi^\top=\Pi$ and $\Pi^2=\Pi$). Then for any $D\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\mathrm{out}}\times d_{\mathrm{in}}}$,
\begin{align}
\langle D\Pi,\; D(I-\Pi)\rangle_F &= 0,
\label{eq:appendix-cross0}\\
\|D\|_F^2 &= \|D\Pi\|_F^2 + \|D(I-\Pi)\|_F^2.
\label{eq:appendix-pythag}
\end{align}

\paragraph{Proof.}
Using $\langle A,B\rangle_F=\mathrm{tr}(A^\top B)$,
\[
\langle D\Pi,\,D(I-\Pi)\rangle_F
= \mathrm{tr}\!\big(\Pi D^\top D(I-\Pi)\big)
= \mathrm{tr}\!\big(\Pi D^\top D - \Pi D^\top D\Pi\big)
= \mathrm{tr}\!\big(\Pi D^\top D(I-\Pi)\big),
\]
but since $\Pi(I-\Pi)=0$, the quantity is zero. Expanding $\|D\|_F^2=\|D\Pi + D(I-\Pi)\|_F^2$ and using \Cref{eq:appendix-cross0} gives \Cref{eq:appendix-pythag}. \hfill$\square$

Lemma A.2 applied to $\Pi_{S_k^t}$ gives the orthogonal split
\[
\|D_{k,i}^t\|_F^2
= \|D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}}\|_F^2 + \|R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}\|_F^2.
\]
Next, by construction $P_{k,i}^t$ is obtained by orthogonalizing $\hat P_{k,i}^t$ against $S_k^t$ and re-orthonormalizing:
\[
P_{k,i}^t = \operatorname{orth}\!\big((I-\Pi_{S_k^t})\hat P_{k,i}^t\big).
\]
Thus $\mathrm{span}(P_{k,i}^t)\subseteq \mathrm{range}(I-\Pi_{S_k^t})$, implying $(S_k^t)^\top P_{k,i}^t = 0$ and $\Pi_{S_k^t}\Pi_{P_{k,i}^t}=0$. This is the same projector-orthogonalization principle used in Gram--Schmidt-style subspace discrimination (This analysis is heavily inspired by the analysis found in UNLEARN~\citet{lizzo2024unlearn}).

Finally, applying Lemma A.2 again within the residual (with $\Pi=\Pi_{P_{k,i}^t}$ and $D=R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}$) yields
\[
\|R_{k,i}^{t,(0)}\|_F^2
= \|D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}}\|_F^2 + \|R_{k,i}^t\|_F^2,
\]
and combining the two Pythagorean identities gives the three-way energy decomposition
\begin{equation}
\|D_{k,i}^t\|_F^2
= \|D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{glob}}\|_F^2
+ \|D_{k,i}^{t,\mathrm{priv}}\|_F^2
+ \|R_{k,i}^t\|_F^2.
\label{eq:appendix-threeway}
\end{equation}
Equation \Cref{eq:appendix-threeway} implies that the energy fractions in \Cref{eq:fractions} sum to $1$ (up to numerical error), and makes explicit that increasing $r_g$ and/or $r_c$ can only decrease residual energy, since each basis is chosen by a best-approximation criterion (Lemma A.1).

\subsection{Operator form of the structured delta}
\label{sec:appendix-operator}

We derive \Cref{eq:operator_view} directly from the definitions. Suppressing indices $(k,i,t)$ for readability, write $\Pi_S$ for the shared projector and $\Pi_P$ for the (post-orthogonalization) private projector.

From \Cref{eq:glob_comp,eq:resid0,eq:priv_comp}, we have
\[
D^{\mathrm{glob}} = D\Pi_S,\qquad
D^{\mathrm{priv}} = D(I-\Pi_S)\Pi_P.
\]
From \Cref{eq:final_resid}, the residual is
\[
R = D - D\Pi_S - D(I-\Pi_S)\Pi_P
  = D(I-\Pi_S)(I-\Pi_P),
\]
where the last equality uses distributivity and $\Pi_S(I-\Pi_S)=0$. Plugging these into \Cref{eq:structured_delta} gives
\begin{align*}
\tilde D
&= D(I-\Pi_S)\Pi_P \;+\; \beta_g D\Pi_S \;+\; \beta_r D(I-\Pi_S)(I-\Pi_P)\\
&= \beta_r D \;+\; (\beta_g-\beta_r)D\Pi_S \;+\; (1-\beta_r)D(I-\Pi_S)\Pi_P,
\end{align*}
which matches \Cref{eq:operator_view}. In particular, setting $\beta_g=\beta_r=1$ recovers $\tilde D=D$ and thus FedAvg on banked keys (as noted in \Cref{sec:structured-agg}).
